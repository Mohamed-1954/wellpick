<aside class="short-answer">
  <p>
    Write one page with four parts: what data may go into AI tools, which
    tools are approved, what a person checks before AI output reaches a
    client, and what you tell clients. Review it twice a year. It will not
    make you compliant on its own, but it turns habits into decisions.
  </p>
</aside>

Somebody on your team has already pasted a client brief into an AI chat to "tidy it up". Probably several people, several times. That is not a scandal. It is a sign that AI is now part of how client work gets done, and that the rules are still in people's heads.

A one-page policy moves them onto paper. It is not a legal document. It is a shared answer to "can I put this in there?".

If you want a deeper framework, NIST's AI Risk Management Framework, released on 26 January 2023 and "intended for voluntary use", is a free starting point, and NIST added a Generative AI Profile (NIST-AI-600-1) in July 2024. For a five-person studio, one page is a more realistic first step.

## Part 1: what data may go in

Sort client data into three levels and say what each level may do:

| Level | Examples | AI tools |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Public | Published website copy, public product pages | Any approved tool |
| Client work | Briefs, drafts, internal notes about a project | Approved tools with business terms only |
| Sensitive | Contracts, financials, personal data about people, passwords | Not in AI tools, unless a named person approves a specific case |

The table matters more than the wording. When someone hesitates, they check the row.

## Part 2: which tools are approved

List the AI tools your team may use for client work, and for each one note:

- **The account type.** Personal free accounts and business accounts often come with different terms about how your inputs are used. Read the current terms for each tool you approve.
- **Who owns the account.** A business account the company controls, not a personal login.
- **Where AI is built into tools you already use.** Many of them now include it. Fillout has an AI form builder. Softr has an AI app builder. Noloco's workflows include AI text actions such as summarise and correct grammar. These count too, because client data passes through them.

Keep the list short. A short list gets followed.

## Part 3: what a person checks

AI output that reaches a client needs a named human check. Write down what the check covers:

- **Facts and numbers.** Prices, dates, names and anything quoted.
- **Tone and promises.** AI drafts can promise things your contract does not.
- **Other clients' details.** A summary built from several sources can mix them.
- **Originality where it matters.** If you sell original creative work, say how AI may and may not be used in it.

A useful rule: if you would not send it unread from a junior colleague, do not send it unread from a model.

## Part 4: what you tell clients

Clients increasingly ask. Decide your answer before they do:

- Do you use AI tools in their work? For what?
- Does their data go into those tools? At which level?
- Who reviews the output?

Some clients will ask you to keep their data out of AI tools entirely. Note those clients in your records, so the request survives a staff change.

## Keep it alive

Put a review date on the page, six months out. Tools change their features and terms often. A policy written in spring may be out of date by autumn.

When AI is built into the portal or form tools you choose, the same questions apply to those features. Our post on [AI app builders for client portals](/blog/ai-app-builders-client-portals/) covers how to check what an AI builder configured on your behalf.
