You searched for a software comparison and landed on a page with a winner, a big button and a discount code. Someone probably gets paid when you click that button. That is not automatically a problem. It is a reason to read the page differently.

This post explains how the money works, using the three vendors we cover as examples, and gives you a quick way to judge any review site, including this one.

## How the money works

Software companies run affiliate programs. A publisher joins, gets a tracked link, and earns a share of what referred customers pay. The terms for the three tools we cover, as their program pages describe them:

| Vendor | Commission | Duration |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Softr | 25% on new customers | 12 months |
| Noloco | 20% on subscriptions | 12 months on the program page; the partner terms list a second year at 10% |
| Fillout | 30% recurring on paid plans by new users | Up to 12 months |

Notice what this means. A review site earns more when you choose a more expensive plan, and more over a year than on the first payment. The incentive is real, and it is built into the business.

## What the FTC expects

The US Federal Trade Commission's Endorsement Guides apply to affiliate links. Its plain-language FAQ says an affiliate marketer should "disclose your relationship to the retailer clearly and conspicuously on your site", and that "the closer the disclosure is to your recommendation, the better."

The FAQ also says what does not work:

- "Affiliate link" by itself may not be understood as meaning the writer gets paid.
- A "buy now" button is not a disclosure.
- A disclosure only at the very bottom of a page can be missed.

A suggested wording from the FAQ is simple: "I get commissions for purchases made through links in this post."

## Five checks for any review

**1. Find the disclosure.** Is there one, and is it near the recommendation or hidden at the bottom? Does it say plainly that the site may be paid?

**2. Look for dates.** Software prices and plans change often. A review that never says when its facts were verified may describe a product that no longer exists in that form.

**3. Follow two claims to their source.** Pick a price and a limit. Does the page link to the vendor's own pricing or docs? Do they match today?

**4. Look for a "do not buy" case.** An honest review tells some readers to choose something else, or nothing. If every reader is sent to the same button, the page is a sales page.

**5. Check what the reviewer claims to have done.** "We tested" means something specific. If a site says it tested a product, look for evidence of it: its own screenshots, a described setup, results. If it only read the vendor's pages, it should say so.

## How Wellpick handles it

We hold ourselves to the same checks. Today, no affiliate links are active on this site: every product link goes to the vendor's official site with no affiliate identifier, and nothing here earns a commission. All three vendors run the programs above, and our [affiliate disclosure](/disclosure/) shows their terms and what would change if we joined. It is linked from every page.

Prices and limits come from dated data with sources listed at the end of each article. When the evidence points to a free plan, a cheaper tool, or buying nothing, we say that. And where we have only read vendor documentation, we say that too, as we do in posts like this one.
