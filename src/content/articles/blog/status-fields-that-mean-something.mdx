Open any client portal and the status is the first thing a client reads. "In progress". "Review". "On hold". Most of them say very little.

"In progress" does not tell the client whether they need to do anything. "Review" does not say who is reviewing. "On hold" invites a phone call. A status field that means something saves more messages than any notification.

## The test for a good status

Every status should complete this sentence:

> Right now, **[someone]** is **[doing something]**, and the next step is **[a handoff]**.

If you cannot finish it, the status is a mood, not a state.

| Vague | Specific |
| --- | --- |
| In progress | We are designing (next: your review) |
| Review | Waiting on your feedback |
| On hold | Paused until you send the brand files |
| Done | Delivered, final files in your folder |

## How many statuses

Five or six is plenty for most service work. One per handoff:

1. **Brief received.** We are reading it.
2. **Scoping.** We are preparing a quote.
3. **Waiting on you.** A decision, an approval or files.
4. **In production.** We are making the work.
5. **In your review.** You are checking a draft.
6. **Delivered.** Final files are with you.

More than that and two statuses start to mean the same thing. Fewer and "in progress" swallows half the project.

## One field, two labels

Your team may want internal detail: "Internal QA", "Waiting on contractor". Clients do not need it. Resist the urge to add a second status field for clients. Someone will forget to update it.

Instead, keep one status field and map each value to a client-facing label. "Internal QA" and "Waiting on contractor" can both show as "In production". How you do this depends on the tool: a formula field that turns the internal value into a client label is one common way.

Then make sure clients cannot see the internal field itself. Noloco's docs separate visibility (what a page shows) from permissions (what a user can reach). Hiding a column on a page is visibility. Use a field permission if the internal value must stay private.

## Let the status drive the board and the messages

Once the status means something, use it everywhere:

- **The board.** Noloco's docs define a kanban board by its grouping field, "typically a Single Option field like Status". Your status becomes the columns, and dragging a card to another column updates it.
- **The portal.** Clients see the same columns in their own view, filtered to their records.
- **Notifications.** A workflow triggered when the status changes to "Waiting on you" can email the client. That one message replaces a lot of chasing. Count it against your plan's [workflow runs](/blog/workflow-runs-explained/).

## Test it with a client's eyes

Take three real projects and read only their status, as a client would. For each one, ask: do I know whether I need to act? If the answer is "it depends", the status needs a sharper name.
