## The short definition

A client portal is a private web app for your clients. Each client signs in and sees the part of your business that belongs to them. Nothing else.

In practice that means a few pages built on top of records you already keep:

- **Projects** with a status, a next step and a date.
- **Files** they sent you and files you delivered.
- **Invoices** and what is still due.
- **Approvals** waiting on them, such as a quote, a design or a contract.
- **A form** to ask for something new.

The key word is "own". A portal is not a page every client can see. It is the same page, filled with different records depending on who signed in.

## What a portal replaces

Most small businesses already do the job a portal does, by hand:

| Today | With a portal |
| --- | --- |
| A weekly status email you write for each client | A status field the client reads when they want |
| A shared folder per client, with its own link | A files list filtered to the signed-in client |
| "Can you resend the invoice?" | An invoices page with the PDF and the amount due |
| Approvals buried in an email thread | A button on the record, with a date stamp |

The gain is not the software. It is that the client stops waiting for you to tell them something that already exists in your records.

## How the "own records" part works

Every portal tool needs a rule that connects a signed-in person to their records. The common pattern is a company link: each user belongs to a company, each project belongs to a company, and the rule says "show projects where the company matches the user's company".

Softr calls these global data restrictions, set per user group. Noloco calls them record-level permissions, set per role, for example `Company` equals `User's Company`. Airtable's own Portals add-on shares Airtable interfaces with outside users who sign in to Airtable.

The rule is the whole product. A portal with a wrong rule is worse than no portal, because it shows one client another client's work. We explain the rules in more detail in [record-level permissions without code](/blog/record-level-permissions-explained/).

## Signs you do not need one yet

A portal is a small software project. It needs setup, testing and someone to keep the records current. Skip it for now if:

- **You have a handful of clients and they rarely ask for updates.** A short weekly email is cheaper.
- **Your records are not up to date today.** A portal shows stale data faster, not better.
- **Clients only need to receive files.** A shared folder with clear names does that job.
- **You cannot name the action clients will take.** Reading a status is useful. Approving, uploading or paying is where portals earn their cost.

## Signs you do

- Clients ask where things are, and the answer is already in your records.
- Several people at each client need the same information.
- You chase approvals by email and lose track of who said yes.
- Staff spend real time writing updates that repeat the project tracker.
